# MMP: Why Your Vote Works Weird (And How to Use It)

> **The Brief** — Explainer Series  
> **Reading time:** 8 minutes  
> **The short version:** You get two votes. One decides who runs the country. One decides who answers your angry emails. Here's why that matters.

---

## Two Votes, Two Purposes

Under MMP — Mixed Member Proportional — you fill out two circles on election day:

**Party Vote:** "Which party should form the Government?"  
**Electorate Vote:** "Who should be my local MP?"

The party vote is the big one. It determines how many seats each party gets in Parliament. Get 30% of the party vote, get roughly 30% of the seats (about 36 MPs).

The electorate vote chooses your local representative. There are 72 electorates — 65 general, 7 Māori. Win the most votes in your area, win the seat.

Simple enough. But why do we do it this way?

---

## The Problem with the Old System

Before 1996, New Zealand used First Past the Post (FPP), same as the UK and US. Whoever got the most votes in each area won. Simple. Also fundamentally broken.

**Three elections broke the system:**

- **1978:** Labour got more votes. National formed Government.
- **1981:** Labour got more votes. National formed Government again.
- **1993:** Labour got more votes. National stayed in power anyway.

Voters were tired of backing losers who won. In 1993, 54% voted to switch to MMP. The first MMP election was 1996.

---

## How Seats Actually Get Allocated

**Total MPs:** 120

**Electorate seats (72):** Win your local area, you're in. These are decided purely by the electorate vote.

**List seats (48):** These "top up" the results to match party vote proportions.

**Example — 2023 Election:**
- National got 38% of party vote → entitled to ~46 seats
- They won 43 electorate seats → got 3 list MPs to reach 46

- Labour got 26.9% of party vote → entitled to ~32 seats  
- They won 17 electorate seats → got 15 list MPs

- ACT got 8.6% of party vote → entitled to ~10 seats
- They won 1 electorate seat → got 9 list MPs

The system corrects itself. If a party wins tons of electorates but few party votes, they keep the electorates but get few or no list seats. If a party gets lots of party votes but few electorates, list seats make up the difference.

---

## The Weird Stuff (And Why It Happens)

### Overhang

Sometimes a party wins more electorate seats than their party vote entitles them to. They keep the extras. Parliament gets bigger temporarily.

**Real example — 2020:** Labour won 46 electorate seats but their party vote only justified ~43 total seats. Parliament expanded to 121 MPs until the next election.

This has happened four times since 1996. It's not a bug. It's MMP making sure local winners keep their seats.

### The 5% Threshold (Or One Electorate)

To get into Parliament, a party must either:
- Get 5% of the party vote, OR
- Win at least one electorate seat

**2020:** Greens got 7.9% → in. NZ First got 7.6% → in. New Conservative got 2.6% and no electorates → out, despite 62,000 votes.

**2017:** Greens got 6.3% → in. TOP got 2.4% → out.

The threshold keeps Parliament manageable but frustrates small parties. It's been controversial since day one.

### Split Voting

About 30% of voters split their ticket — different party and electorate votes. It's not strategic. It's just... allowed. You can support Labour nationally but like your local National MP. Many people do.

**2020 example:** Chloe Swarbrick won Auckland Central for Greens despite Labour winning the party vote there comfortably. Voters wanted Labour in power but Chloe as their MP.

---

## Coalitions: The Price of Proportionality

Under FPP, one party often got 50%+ and ruled alone. Under MMP, that's rare. Since 1996, only one party has ever won an outright majority: Labour in 2020 (barely, with 50.01%).

**How Government Formation Actually Works:**

1. **Election night:** We know the numbers
2. **Negotiation phase:** Biggest party talks to smaller parties
3. **Coalition deal:** Who gets what ministries, what policies happen
4. **Confidence vote:** New Government proves they have 61+ seats

**The deals:**
- **1996-1998:** National + NZ First (collapsed spectacularly)
- **1999-2008:** Labour + Alliance/Progressive + Greens (confidence)
- **2008-2017:** National + ACT + United Future + Māori
- **2017-2020:** Labour + NZ First + Greens (confidence)  
- **2020-2023:** Labour + Greens (confidence)
- **2023-present:** National + ACT + NZ First

**Why this matters:** Winston Peters held up Government formation for four weeks in 2017. He got Deputy Prime Minister and control of foreign affairs. ACT got regulatory reform and David Seymour as Associate Education Minister. Small parties leverage their seats for real power.

---

## Should You Try to Game It?

Some voters attempt "strategic" voting:
- "I'll party-vote Greens but electorate-vote Labour to stop National winning my seat"
- "I'll support a minor party so they get over 5%"

**Our view:** Don't overthink it. The party vote determines Government. The electorate vote determines your local MP. Splitting is fine if you genuinely prefer different options. But trying to "game" MMP usually backfires or makes no difference.

The system is surprisingly robust. Just vote for what you believe.

---

## The Honest Assessment: Pros and Cons

### What Works
✅ **Fairness:** Parliament looks like the electorate (roughly)  
✅ **No wasted votes:** 7.6% gets you 7.6% of seats, not zero  
✅ **More choice:** Split tickets, support small parties, still matter  
✅ **Cooperation required:** Parties have to work together  
✅ **Diversity:** More women, Māori, and minority MPs than under FPP

### What Doesn't  
❌ **Complexity:** Two votes confuses people (especially new voters)  
❌ **Kingmakers:** Small parties extract concessions disproportionate to their size  
❌ **Instability:** Coalitions collapse (1998, almost 2005, 2017 was messy)  
❌ **List MPs:** Not directly accountable to voters, chosen by party hierarchy  
❌ **Threshold unfairness:** 4.9% gets you nothing; 5.1% gets you seats

## Common Myths (Corrected)

**Myth: "The party with the most votes automatically wins Government"**  
**Reality:** They get first shot, but if others combine against them, they can be blocked. Hasn't happened yet, but constitutionally possible.

**Myth: "Electorate votes matter more"**  
**Reality:** Party votes decide who forms Government. Electorate votes decide local representation. Different purposes, equal importance.

**Myth: "Small parties have too much power"**  
**Reality:** They have leverage in coalition talks, but big parties still dominate legislation. ACT's 8.6% can't override National's 38%.

**Myth: "MMP lets losers win"**  
**Reality:** It prevents winners from taking all. 38% support shouldn't give 100% power. That's the point.

---

## What Actually Happens to Weird Votes

**You vote for a party that gets 4.9% and no electorates:**  
→ They're out. Your party vote redistributes proportionally to parties that made it.

**You split your vote — Labour party, National electorate:**  
→ Totally valid. About 1 in 3 voters do this. Both votes count.

**You only fill out the electorate vote:**  
→ Your party vote is blank and doesn't count.

**You deliberately spoil your ballot:**  
→ No one cares. Spoiled ballots are recorded but ignored.

---

## The Bottom Line

MMP isn't perfect. The 5% threshold is arbitrary. List MPs aren't directly elected. Coalitions can be messy.

But it beats the alternative. Your vote actually matters proportionally. Small parties can win representation. Parliament looks more like New Zealand.

**What to do:**
- **Party vote:** The party whose vision you support nationally
- **Electorate vote:** The person you want answering your emails locally

They can be the same. They can be different. It's up to you.
